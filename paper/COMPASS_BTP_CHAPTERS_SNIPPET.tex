\chapter{Introduction}

Atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide a quantitative bridge between chemical detail and biophysical behavior, but the timescale gap between integrator step and biologically relevant rare events makes naive long-time sampling infeasible for almost all interesting systems.\cite{lindorff2011how,chodera2014markov}

Adaptive MD research has therefore concentrated on where to place the next short run when data are gathered on \emph{one} target system; comparatively less attention has gone to the practical scenario in which a long trajectory already exists for a \emph{related} source (parent fold, alternate sequence, or chemically perturbed state) and one wishes to restart the target more intelligently than generic frontier rules allow.
Yet related structures often retain \emph{partial} continuity in slow motion and in coarse organization of configurational space, enough that a source free-energy \emph{map}, treated as a fallible atlas rather than as exact truth, can bias \emph{which} unexplored regions deserve finite sampling budget on the target.\cite{voelz2013examining,zimmerman2017prediction,pantsar2018assessment}
The difficulty is deformation: mutations can reroute kinetics, bury former pathways, or open target-specific channels, so any imported atlas must be paired with mechanisms that limit blind allegiance.

The value of a source atlas depends strongly on the dimensionality of the collective variable.
With a one-dimensional coordinate (as in a simple helix--coil transition), only two frontiers exist: the two ends of the pathway.
A sampler with any reasonable coordinate will make progress by advancing toward whichever end is less explored, and the main contribution of source-system data is to sharpen the coordinate itself and reveal barrier heights along the way.
The picture changes qualitatively in two or more dimensions.
Data-driven coordinates such as TICA or RAVE routinely produce two-dimensional (or higher) latent spaces where occupied basins are surrounded by a large boundary surface: many distinct frontier directions compete simultaneously for limited budget.
Here a source map contributes something qualitatively more: it signals \emph{which} frontiers are worth entering (where source-supported mass is concentrated, where barriers are high, and where the path deadends), so that budget is not wasted on directions the target system is unlikely to populate.
This directional guidance, built from prior MD of a related system, becomes more valuable the higher the dimensionality of the coordinate.

Generic adaptive heuristics emphasize novelty and frontiers, but not every frontier is equally informative for a mutant.
A subtler failure is exploration that accumulates \emph{coverage} or geometric spread without tracking where the target ensemble places mass, steering into latent regions that are easy to separate yet poorly populated at equilibrium, or following routes the mutation has closed while missing newly opened ones.
\emph{COMPASS} (Conformation-Map Optimized Mutant Prioritized Adaptive Sequential Sampling) addresses both concerns: it scores each candidate with named components (source-shaped density deficit, boundary trust, redundancy, and phased weights), so that decisions remain interpretable and successes or failures can be attributed to concrete mechanisms rather than to a monolithic adaptive heuristic.

This report introduces COMPASS as a \emph{method} and evaluates it through a retrospective archive-replay benchmark where candidates are generated via structural equivalence from the target trajectory library, selecting chunks without committing to new MD simulations.
We evaluate five policies ranging from a single uninterrupted walk to the full COMPASS scoring, under systematic ablations of chunk length, collective-variable type, and prior quality.
Observable estimates from the selected frames are corrected for adaptive sampling bias using reference-reweighted importance sampling.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.6ex]
\item \textbf{Interpretable prior-aware acquisition.} Named scoring terms make the policy transparent: when COMPASS outperforms or underperforms, the gain or loss can be traced to a specific component (deficit, trust, novelty, redundancy) rather than attributed to ``adaptive sampling'' as a black box.
\item \textbf{Objective-specific latent roles.} Ablations across TICA and SPIB priors in one and two dimensions separate exploration utility from equilibrium density guidance, showing that the choice of collective variable should match the scientific objective.
\item \textbf{Bias-corrected observables.} Reference-reweighted importance sampling recovers equilibrium helix and open fractions from adaptively seeded chunks without requiring unbiased trajectory weights.
\item \textbf{Source--target coverage correlation.} Coverage accumulated in the A10 source space predicts coverage in the A8Q2 target space, providing a practical proxy for online assessment before the target reference is available.
\end{enumerate}

The report is organized as follows: Chapter~\ref{chap:lit} surveys related work on adaptive sampling and learned collective variables; Chapter~\ref{chap:methods} presents theory and methodology; Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reports results and discussion; Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions} gives a summary and concluding remarks.

\chapter{Literature Survey}\label{chap:lit}

Rare biophysical rearrangements unfold on time scales orders of magnitude beyond routine MD budgets, so the field has long relied on enhanced sampling, collective variables, and coarse-grained kinetic models that either bias exploration along chosen coordinates or reweight trajectories to recover correct statistics.\cite{lindorff2011how,chodera2014markov,laio2002escaping,barducci2008well,tribello2014plumed2}
Markov state models (MSMs) summarize long atomistic trajectories as transitions among metastable sets and have become a standard route from raw MD to interpretable kinetics and experimentally testable predictions.\cite{prinz2011markov,husic2018markov,plattner2017complete}
MSM construction is itself a sampling problem: poor coverage of slow barriers produces biased counts; conversely, once a reasonable state space is in hand, MSMs can guide where additional data are most informative.

A complementary line of work treats sampling as \emph{sequential decision-making}: rather than extending a single trajectory indefinitely, one launches many short segments and uses online estimators to choose the next starting conditions.
MSM-based adaptive protocols target regions of high model uncertainty in slow modes or in quantities tied to experimental observables.\cite{bowman2010adaptive}
The FAST framework makes the exploration--exploitation trade-off explicit by scoring candidate launches with respect to user goals and model error.\cite{zimmerman2015fast}
Separately, weighted-ensemble and related path-sampling schemes maintain replicated branches with weights chosen so that rare-event statistics (and, with care, rates) can be estimated without artificial biasing potentials along a single chosen coordinate.\cite{huber1996we,zuckerman2017weighted}
Recent weighted-ensemble variants combine these ideas with modern CV learning to improve scalability on biomolecular benchmarks.\cite{yang2024werl,leung2025dlwe}

Choosing \emph{where} to act next presupposes a coordinate system in which progress and redundancy are recognizable.
Variational formulations such as time-lagged independent component analysis (TICA) estimate slow linear projections from equilibrium time series and remain a backbone of MSM pipelines.\cite{perezhernandez2013tica,schwantes2013improvements,scherer2015pyemma}
Nonlinear and neural CVs widen the expressive range: deep time-lagged autoencoders,\cite{wehmeyer2018time} VAMPnets,\cite{mardt2018vampnets} nonlinear RAVE-like models,\cite{ribeiro2018rave} and state-predictive information bottlenecks\cite{tishby2000information,wang2021spib} can capture curvature and state-separation patterns that linear maps miss.\cite{noe2017collective}
Coupling learned coordinates to reinforcement-learning style policies or accelerated MD loops is now widespread in proof-of-principle peptide and protein workflows.\cite{shamsi2018reap,kleiman2022multiagent,lee2019deepdrivemd}
Turning these ideas into reproducible pipelines additionally relies on community simulation and MSM-estimation tools, for example GPU-accelerated MD engines, validated biomolecular force fields, and libraries such as PyEMMA that make variational slow-coordinate estimation routine.\cite{eastman2024openmm8,huang2017charmm36m,kim2015charmmgui,scherer2015pyemma}

Across much of this literature, the surrogate coordinate or encoder is trained and deployed on statistically related data from \emph{the same} molecular system whose kinetics one ultimately wants.
For protein engineering and mutant studies, however, simulations of a parent scaffold or alternate sequence already exist before a new variant is modeled in depth, and transferable priors are attractive on cost grounds alone.\cite{voelz2013examining,zimmerman2017prediction,pantsar2018assessment}
Yet transfer also amplifies ambiguity: latent directions need not remain globally faithful across mutations, yet may still correlate with chemically meaningful observables.
This work contributes on two levels: it specifies \emph{COMPASS}, a sequential acquisition policy that injects transferred slow-coordinate structure into restart decisions under explicit trust controls, and it presents a systematic ablation benchmark evaluating the policy across latent variables, chunk lengths, and prior quality before costly online MD.

\chapter{Materials and Methods}\label{chap:methods}

\section{Theory and Background}

\subsection{Adaptive Sampling as Sequential Acquisition}

We frame any adaptive MD scheme as a sequential acquisition problem on a candidate set $\mathcal{C}$ of structures or trajectory chunks.
At step $t$, given a history $\mathcal{H}_t = \{c_1,\ldots,c_{t-1}\}$ of previously selected candidates and an evaluation metric $\mathcal{M}$, the policy selects $c_t \in \mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{H}_t$ to maximize a scoring function $S_t(c)$.
The goal is to reduce the expected loss after adding $c_t$, $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H}_t \cup \{c_t\})]$.
In online MD, $\mathcal{C}$ corresponds to candidate restart points and $\mathcal{M}$ measures observable error or distributional accuracy after launching a new trajectory.
In the retrospective benchmark used here, $\mathcal{C}$ is expanded from visited frames via structural equivalence neighbors in the precomputed target trajectory, and $\mathcal{M}$ is computed from the union of selected chunk frames.

The retrospective setting requires no new MD simulations: all candidate segments come from a precomputed target trajectory, so the entire benchmark runs without any simulation cost.
This makes it possible to compare many policies, latent spaces, budgets, and prior variants rapidly and reproducibly, which is a significant practical advantage when each prospective run would otherwise take days of wall-clock time.
The structure of the decision problem is preserved: at each step the policy must commit to one segment without knowing the future, exactly as in online MD.
A critical distinction from a plain library replay is that the candidate pool at each step is constructed \emph{online} from the visited set expanded through structural neighbors, mirroring the fact that in real MD one can only restart from physically accessible states.
A policy that performs well retrospectively on a faithful target library is a necessary condition for performing well online, and a policy that fails retrospectively is unlikely to recover in a prospective setting.

\subsection{Slow-Coordinate Priors via TICA}

Throughout this work, TICA supplies only a \emph{reference occupancy}: project the A10 source onto leading time-lagged independent components,\cite{perezhernandez2013tica,schwantes2013improvements,scherer2015pyemma} histogram the projections, smooth with unit pseudo-counts, and read bin weights $p_s(b)$.
The symmetric time-lagged covariance estimator and generalized eigenproblem are standard (Supporting Information).
Histogramming yields $p_s(b)$ for prior-corruption tests (Section~\ref{sec:prior_quality}).
We do \emph{not} claim that A10 TICA is a global kinetic model of A8Q2; we ask whether it is useful as a transferred density prior for acquisition.

\subsection{State-Predictive Priors via SPIB}

SPIB encoders\cite{wang2021spib,tishby2000information} emphasize predictive dynamics rather than equilibrium density in latent space.
Using SPIB like a TICA histogram prior conflates ``interesting transitions'' with ``where equilibrium mass should live,'' a mismatch the results make explicit.
Variational training details appear in Supporting Information \eqref{eq:si_spib_loss}; here SPIB is treated as a \emph{discovery} coordinate, not a thermodynamic density estimator.

\subsection{Source-Prior--Deficit Acquisition}
\label{sec:theory_score}

The central idea of COMPASS is a \emph{source-prior--deficit} score.
At each step $t$, we track which latent bin each new segment \emph{enters} (its decision bin), rather than pooling every frame inside it.
Let $n_t(b)$ count the number of segments that have entered bin $b$ so far; the acquired distribution is $\hat{p}_t(b)=n_t(b)/\sum_{b'} n_t(b')$.
The policy then asks: which bins still have source mass that the current sample under-represents?
Bins where $p_s(b)$ is high but $\hat{p}_t(b)$ is low should be visited preferentially.
A direct linear deficit works but lets the densest source bins dominate every selection step; the information-gain form below dampens this by progressively discounting bins as they fill up. The raw deficit
\begin{equation}
    d_t(b) = \max\!\left[p_s(b) - \hat{p}_t(b),\,0\right]
    \label{eq:raw_deficit}
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{loe}{eqloe}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}Raw source-prior deficit}
flags bins where source-supported mass is currently under-represented in the selected sample. However, the raw deficit scales linearly with $p_s$ and saturates slowly at high-density bins, so a few dominant bins can monopolize the score. We therefore use an information-gain form
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    d^{IG}_t(b) &= \frac{N_t(b)}{1+\lambda\,p_s(b)},\\
    N_t(b) &= p_s(b)\,\max\!\left[1-\frac{\hat{p}_t(b)}{\max(p_s(b),10^{-12})},\,0\right].
\end{split}
    \label{eq:ig_deficit}
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{loe}{eqloe}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}Information-gain deficit}
$N_t(b)$ is the source mass in bin $b$ scaled by how under-filled it remains; the denominator $1+\lambda p_s(b)$ progressively discounts dense bins as they are partially visited, preventing a few high-mass bins from monopolizing the selection at every step. $\lambda=20$ throughout.
The final score combines this deficit with frontier, trust, and novelty signals (each normalized to $[0,1]$ across candidates at that step), then multiplies by a bin-fatigue factor that reduces the score of repeatedly chosen bins:
\begin{equation}
    S_t(c) = \frac{1}{1+n_{b(c)}}\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k(t)\,z_k(c),
    \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{loe}{eqloe}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}COMPASS scalar acquisition score}
\noindent
where $b(c)$ is the candidate's decision bin and $n_{b(c)}$ is its visit count before step~$t$.
The \emph{redundancy} component uses
$z_{\mathrm{red}}(c)=\exp\bigl(-d_{\min}^2(c)/\kappa\bigr)$,
with $d_{\min}(c)$ the Euclidean distance in the latent used for frontier scoring to the nearest prior decision point, and $\kappa=0.02$ in latent units squared.
Density terms track the prior; frontier and novelty terms do not, so failures can be traced to named components rather than to ``adaptive sampling'' as a black box.

\subsection{Reweighted Observable Estimation}
\label{sec:theory_reweight}

Adaptive restart policies introduce a non-uniform sampling bias: regions from which many restarts were launched are over-represented in the selected frames relative to their true equilibrium weight.
Within each chunk, however, the dynamics are unbiased (the archived trajectory used no biasing potential), so the bias enters only through the choice of starting points.

We correct for this bias via \emph{reference-reweighted importance sampling}.
Let $b(f)$ denote the latent bin of frame $f$, and let $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(b)$ be the equilibrium bin probability estimated from the full reference long-run trajectory.
Let $\pi_{\mathrm{sample}}(b)=\#\{f\in\mathcal{H}_t: b(f)=b\}/|\mathcal{H}_t|$ be the empirical bin frequency in the selected frames.
The importance weight for bin $b$ is
\begin{equation}
    w(b) = \frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(b)}{\max\!\left(\pi_{\mathrm{sample}}(b),\, 1/|\mathcal{H}_t|\right)},
    \qquad \hat{w}(b) = \frac{w(b)}{\sum_{b'}w(b')}.
    \label{eq:reweight}
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{loe}{eqloe}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}Reference-reweighted importance sampling estimator}
The reweighted helix fraction is then $\hat{f}_{\mathrm{helix}}=\sum_b \hat{w}(b)\,f_{\mathrm{helix}}^{\mathrm{ref}}(b)$, where $f_{\mathrm{helix}}^{\mathrm{ref}}(b)$ is the per-bin helix fraction from the reference trajectory.
This estimator is equivalent to MBAR with zero biasing potential: it recovers $f_{\mathrm{helix}}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ exactly when $\pi_{\mathrm{sample}}=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, and reweights without transition matrix estimation (unlike MSM-based approaches), making it stable even for short acquisition budgets.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Study Design and Scope}

The central question is whether information from an A10 source trajectory can guide effective adaptive sampling of A8Q2; that is, whether a transferred prior would have selected informative chunks had it been used to seed MD.

To test this before committing to new simulations we use \emph{archive-replay}: the existing A8Q2 trajectory supplies a chunk library, and at each step the policy selects the next chunk by scoring candidates drawn from the structural neighborhood of already-visited frames.
This differs from plain library replay (oracle selection from the entire archive) by restricting candidates to frames \emph{structurally similar} to those already visited, exactly as in real online MD where only states reachable from the current position can be restarted.

Because both COMPASS and the baselines draw from the same archived A8Q2 library, this remains a retrospective benchmark.
Gains over random should be read as acquisition-quality diagnostics on a fixed library and as a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for online wall-clock acceleration.
Transfers were restricted to generic torsional features projected through learned latent maps; helix and open labels were withheld from the policy and used only after selection to evaluate whether latent-guided acquisition preserved physically interpretable populations.

\subsection{Computational Details}

\paragraph{Molecular dynamics.}
The A10 source and A8Q2 target reference trajectories were generated with \textsc{OpenMM}~8\cite{eastman2024openmm8} using explicit solvent.
Simulations used the additive CHARMM36m force field with CHARMM-modified TIP3P water (\textsc{OpenMM} \path{charmm36_2024} files).\cite{huang2017charmm36m,kim2015charmmgui}
Electrostatics used particle-mesh Ewald; nonbonded interactions used a 1.2~nm cutoff with 1.0--1.2~nm switching; hydrogen--heavy-atom and rigid-water constraints were enabled; the timestep was 2~fs with a Langevin middle integrator (friction 1~ps$^{-1}$).
After minimization and equilibration (NVT with peptide restraints, then NPT; full protocol in Appendix~\ref{part:supporting}), production NVT simulations were run at \textbf{330~K} for both peptides so that helix--coil statistics are sampled efficiently on a laboratory-accessible budget while keeping the A10/A8Q2 comparison internally consistent.
Coordinates were saved every 0.1~ns (100~ps) for featurization and chunk construction.

\paragraph{Collective variable training.}
TICA was fit on 40-dimensional backbone sin/cos torsional features from the A10 source trajectory using PyEMMA's symmetric covariance estimator.\cite{scherer2015pyemma}
SPIB encoders were trained in PyTorch on the same A10 feature pipeline with latent dimension one (SPIB-1D) or two (SPIB-2D).
Both TICA and SPIB were trained on A10 and \emph{applied without fine-tuning} to project A8Q2 features.
Helix and open evaluation used native hydrogen-bond counts ($N_{\mathrm{HB}}\geq 3$ helical; $N_{\mathrm{HB}}\leq 1$ open), withheld from the acquisition policy.

\subsection{Transferred Latent Atlases}
\label{sec:tica_atlas}

\subsubsection{TICA Atlases}

A10-fitted TICA mean and eigenvectors project A8Q2 backbone torsions into a shared two-dimensional latent space.
Bin edges for the $20\times 20$ histogram grid use target percentiles (padded 0.5--99.5\% ranges).
A8Q2 TIC-1 carries a strong negative correlation with $N_{\mathrm{HB}}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}b), validating the helix--coil signal retained after mutation.
A one-dimensional slice (TICA-1D) uses only the leading component; TICA-2D uses both.

A10$\rightarrow$A8Q2 formal transfer metrics indicate partial global fidelity: TIC-1 direction overlaps the native slow mode and the transferred coordinate tracks helix content, while TIC-2 is rotated relative to the native A8Q2 second component.
We use the A10 TICA here as an empirical 2D density and observable prior on A8Q2, not as a certified A8Q2 kinetic model.
The one-dimensional case (TICA-1D) provides an important reference: in a 1D helix--coil system there are only two frontiers, so any decent collective variable will direct exploration efficiently by advancing toward the less-visited end.
The additional value of TICA-2D, and of the source prior in general, is directing which of the many 2D frontiers deserves budget.

\subsubsection{SPIB Atlases}

A10-trained SPIB encoders project A8Q2 features into SPIB-1D or SPIB-2D latent coordinates.\cite{wang2021spib}
Because the encoder was also transferred and not retrained on A8Q2, SPIB results are interpreted as tests of discovery behavior rather than as direct estimates of equilibrium thermodynamics on the target.

\subsection{Archive-Replay Benchmark and Equivalence-Cloud Expansion}
\label{sec:methods_equiv}

\paragraph{Structural equivalence.}
The key challenge in building a faithful retrospective benchmark is defining which archived frames are physically accessible from a given visited state.
In real MD, restarting from structure $s$ and advancing by $\Delta t$ produces a distribution of outcomes centered on $s$ due to the stochastic integrator.
We model this by defining a \emph{structural neighborhood} of each visited frame: the $k$ nearest archived frames in a combined hydrogen-bond count and backbone torsion feature space (NHB+torsion hybrid, 50/50 normalized contribution), with $k=40$.

NHB+torsion similarity was chosen because it captures both global secondary-structure identity (NHB) and backbone geometry simultaneously, without requiring expensive all-atom superposition.
A temporal gate (maximum 50~ns separation in the same trajectory segment) prevents neighbors from spanning unphysically long structural excursions.

\paragraph{Candidate pool construction.}
At each step, the visited set $\mathcal{V}_t$ consists of all frames from previously selected chunks.
The candidate pool is the union of $\mathcal{V}_t$ and all structural neighbors of frames in $\mathcal{V}_t$, minus frames already used as seeds.
This pool grows as more regions are explored, mirroring the expanding frontier of real adaptive MD.
The policy scores all candidates and selects the next seed.

\paragraph{Chunk continuation.}
Once seed frame $s$ is selected, the benchmark reads the next $L$ consecutive archived frames (a single uninterrupted chunk of length $L$ frames at 0.1~ns/frame) from that trajectory segment.
All $L$ frames update the visited set and contribute to metric computation.
A total budget of 300~ns is used across all experiments, with metrics logged after every chunk.

\subsection{Policy Hierarchy}
\label{sec:methods_policies}

Five policies are compared, ordered by increasing sophistication.
This ordering is designed to isolate each element of the COMPASS design:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em, labelindent=0pt, itemsep=0.4ex]
    \item[\textbf{P1 -- Single Walk.}]
    One continuous sequential walk through the archived trajectory from a randomly chosen starting frame.
    No restart decisions are made; the policy simply reads the archive in order.
    This is the closest analog to an uninterrupted long MD run and serves as a ceiling on what can be achieved without restart overhead, as well as a sanity check that the archive-replay metrics recover ground-truth observables when the full trajectory is replayed.

    \item[\textbf{P2 -- Independent Replicas.}]
    At the very first step, the candidate pool is expanded from the initial frame using the structural equivalence neighbors.
    All $N$ seeds needed to exhaust the 300~ns budget are selected \emph{uniformly at random from this initial pool and committed at once}.
    Subsequent steps pop from this pre-committed list; the pool is never updated.
    P2 represents launching a fixed set of parallel replicas from diverse structural neighbors of a single initial state, without any adaptive feedback.

    \item[\textbf{P3 -- Uniform Random.}]
    At each step, the candidate pool is rebuilt from the current visited set and a seed is chosen uniformly at random.
    The pool updates iteratively but selection is uninformed: no latent prior or coverage information influences the choice.
    P3 isolates the benefit of iterative candidate-pool expansion over the one-shot initialization of P2.

    \item[\textbf{P4 -- Min-Count Adaptive.}]
    At each step, the candidate pool is rebuilt and each frame is assigned to a latent bin (same grid as the source prior).
    The seed is chosen from the bin with the fewest previous visits, breaking ties uniformly.
    P4 represents classical count-based adaptive sampling\cite{zimmerman2015fast} within the equivalence-cloud framework, without using the source prior.

    \item[\textbf{P5 -- COMPASS.}]
    Full COMPASS scoring (Eq.~\ref{eq:score}) with the three-phase schedule and the transferred A10 prior.
    Deficit, frontier, novelty, boundary, and redundancy components are all active; weights follow the two-phase annealing schedule (Supporting Information Table~\ref{tab:si_policy_weights}).
    P5 is the only policy that uses source-prior information.
\end{description}

The P3$\to$P4 increment isolates the value of \emph{adaptive updating} over random selection.
The P4$\to$P5 increment isolates the value of the \emph{transferred source prior} over count-based adaptivity alone.
The P1 vs.\ P3 comparison exposes the cost of restarts (each restart discards temporal continuity) relative to the exploratory benefit of flexible seed placement.

\subsection{Experimental Presets and Ablations}
\label{sec:methods_presets}

All experiments use a total budget of 300~ns, with metrics recorded after every chunk.
Budget effects can thus be read at any intermediate level from a single run.
A base preset (TICA-2D, 2~ns chunks, no noise, 20 repeats per policy) establishes the main policy comparison.
Three focused ablations vary one parameter at a time:

\paragraph{Ablation A -- Collective variable (CV) type.}
TICA-1D, TICA-2D (base), SPIB-1D, and SPIB-2D are tested at chunk=2~ns, no noise.
This reveals how the dimensionality and objective (equilibrium density vs.\ predictive dynamics) of the CV affect policy quality.
P3 and P2 results are CV-agnostic (no latent prior used) and are reused across CV variants.

\paragraph{Ablation B -- Chunk length.}
Seven chunk sizes spanning two orders of magnitude (0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20~ns) are tested at TICA-2D, no noise.
Short chunks update the candidate pool frequently and explore diverse neighborhoods; long chunks amortize restart overhead but coarsen the grid of available restart points.

\paragraph{Ablation C -- Prior quality.}
Five prior variants are tested at TICA-2D, chunk=2~ns: no noise (base), low noise ($\sigma=0.1\times\max$), medium noise ($\sigma=0.3\times\max$), high noise ($\sigma=1.0\times\max$), and fully uniform (no source information).
This stress-tests how robust COMPASS is to imperfect source priors, motivating the trust-control design.

\paragraph{Efficiency.}
P2 and P3 are CV-agnostic and prior-agnostic: their results are computed once per chunk size and shared across Ablations A and C.
P4 is prior-agnostic: its results are computed once per (CV $\times$ chunk size) and shared across Ablation C.
The total experiment suite comprises approximately 3\,600 archive-replay runs, each completing in a few seconds without any simulation budget, enabling the full ablation grid to be explored feasibly.

\paragraph{Statistical evaluation.}
Each (preset, policy) combination is run with 20 independent random starting seeds to estimate mean and standard deviation.
Observable errors and coverage metrics are aggregated across repeats and reported as mean~$\pm$~standard deviation or as convergence curves with shaded bands.

    The P3$\to$P4 gap isolates the gain from adaptive pool updating; the P4$\to$P5 gap isolates the gain from the transferred source prior.}
    \label{fig:base_convergence}
\end{figure}

The P1 single walk provides the long-budget upper bound on observable recovery: given enough budget, reading the archive sequentially recovers the reference because all frames are eventually selected.
Its short-budget helix-fraction error is moderate ($2.54\% \pm 1.78\%$ at 50~ns) because the single trajectory advances linearly without branching toward helix-rich regions.

P2 independent replicas spreads restarts across the structural neighborhood of the initial state but cannot adapt as new regions are visited.
Coverage saturates early ($54.0\% \pm 7.1\%$ at 100~ns) because all seeds are pre-committed and cluster in the initial neighborhood, with observable error persisting at $3.38\% \pm 1.81\%$ at 100~ns.

P3 uniform random with iterative pool updating outperforms P2 in coverage ($57.5\% \pm 5.5\%$ at 100~ns) because the pool expands as new regions are explored, giving the sampler access to increasingly diverse candidates.
The gap between P2 and P3 quantifies the benefit of iterative candidate-pool expansion; however, P3 observable error ($3.50\% \pm 1.17\%$ at 100~ns) remains comparable to P2 because uniform sampling does not target informationally rich regions.

P4 min-count adaptive achieves substantially lower observable error than P3 ($1.02\% \pm 0.75\%$ vs.\ $3.50\% \pm 1.17\%$ at 100~ns) and higher coverage ($89.8\% \pm 2.8\%$ at 100~ns), demonstrating that bin-level count awareness adds significant value beyond uniform random selection even without a source prior.
At long budgets, P4 achieves near-perfect coverage ($99.2\% \pm 0.9\%$ at 200~ns) and retains low observable error ($1.22\% \pm 0.52\%$).

P5 COMPASS achieves the lowest reweighted observable errors at short-to-medium budgets: $1.09\% \pm 0.66\%$ at 50~ns and $0.63\% \pm 0.43\%$ at 100~ns.
Coverage reaches $93.4\% \pm 2.3\%$ at 100~ns, substantially ahead of P3 ($57.5\%$) and P4 ($89.8\%$) at the same budget.
The source-prior deficit term drives early exploration toward source-supported regions where helix content is concentrated, while the frontier and novelty terms prevent redundancy.
The P4$\to$P5 gap, which isolates the value of the transferred A10 prior, is largest at short budgets: at 50~ns, P5 ($1.09\%$) and P4 ($1.13\%$) are comparable, but P5 reaches the $2\%$ threshold $3.6\times$ faster than P4 on the milestone metric (Section~\ref{sec:results_acceleration}).
At long budgets (200~ns), however, P4 observable error ($1.22\%$) falls below P5 ($2.92\%$) despite near-identical coverage ($99.2\%$ vs.\ $98.8\%$).
This crossover reflects the reweighting correction penalizing the helix-basin over-sampling that persists after the source deficit is resolved: COMPASS continues to over-weight the prior-dense region even when the coverage gap has closed, inflating the importance weights and increasing reweighted error variance at long budgets.

Across the four CV types, COMPASS (P5) achieves reweighted helix-fraction errors at 100~ns of $0.57\%\pm0.48\%$ (TICA-1D), $0.63\%\pm0.43\%$ (TICA-2D), $1.04\%\pm0.91\%$ (SPIB-1D), and $0.65\%\pm0.28\%$ (SPIB-2D).

This reversal is consistent with the known mismatch between SPIB's training objective and the density prior assumed by COMPASS: SPIB encodes \emph{transition-predictive} rather than \emph{density-weighted} information, so the A10 SPIB histogram used as a source prior concentrates on transition-rich rather than equilibrium-dense regions.
Directing acquisition toward those transition-rich bins under SPIB-1D overshoots the equilibrium helix mass and degrades the reweighted estimator.
The SPIB-2D COMPASS result ($0.65\%$) recovers competitive performance, likely because the second dimension partially decouples equilibrium density from transition kinetics, restoring a directional prior that is compatible with the deficit term.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Design interpretation.}
The five-policy hierarchy cleanly separates the contributions of four design elements: pool expansion (P1 vs.\ P3), iterative updating (P2 vs.\ P3), count-based adaptivity (P3 vs.\ P4), and source-prior guidance (P4 vs.\ P5).
Each element adds measurable value for at least one metric and budget level.
The P3$\to$P4 step delivers the largest absolute reduction in observable error ($3.50\%\to1.02\%$ at 100~ns), while the P4$\to$P5 step delivers the most compressed time-to-threshold ($3.6\times\to6.5\times$ speedup for helix error $\leq 2\%$).
The combination in P5 is most consistently beneficial for short-to-medium budgets where the source prior is still directionally informative.

\paragraph{Budget regime dependence.}
A notable feature of the results is the budget-regime interaction: COMPASS outperforms P4 strongly at short-to-medium budgets ($\leq 150$~ns) but P4 achieves lower reweighted helix error at long budgets ($\geq 200$~ns).
At 200~ns both policies achieve near-perfect coverage ($99.2\%$ and $98.8\%$), but the reweighted estimator reveals that COMPASS's continued over-sampling of helix-adjacent bins inflates the importance weights and increases error variance even after coverage has converged.
This suggests a practical refinement: phasing out the source-deficit term once coverage reaches a threshold (e.g., 90\%) and switching to a pure count-balancing schedule for the remaining budget.

\paragraph{1D versus 2D collective variables.}
The measured CV ablation confirms that COMPASS provides density guidance even in 1D ($0.57\%$ vs.\ $1.12\%$ for P4 at 100~ns with TICA-1D) and directional guidance in 2D ($0.63\%$ vs.\ $1.02\%$ with TICA-2D).
The SPIB-1D reversal, where P4 outperforms COMPASS ($0.51\%$ vs.\ $1.04\%$), identifies the case where the prior training objective (transition prediction) is misaligned with the density-deficit acquisition criterion, a mismatch that is suppressed in 2D but dominant in 1D where a single mis-directed prior exhausts the frontier budget.

\paragraph{Reweighting and observable estimation.}
The reference-reweighted estimator (Section~\ref{sec:theory_reweight}) corrects for restart over-sampling before reporting observable errors.
Without this correction, policies that restart many times from the helix basin, because the source prior is concentrated there, will report artificially low helix-fraction error even if they have poor distributional coverage.
The reweighting makes the comparison fair: a policy is credited only if it recovers the correct equilibrium fraction given its selection pattern, not merely if it over-samples the observable-rich region.
The long-budget P4/P5 crossover is a direct consequence of this corrector: it surfaces the over-sampling cost that naive helix-fraction estimates would obscure.

\paragraph{Practical significance.}
The retrospective benchmark answers whether a transferred prior would have been informative had it been used to guide real MD seeding.
Gains here are a necessary but not sufficient condition for online acceleration, because the archive imposes a fixed pool and does not include the variance from actual integrator dynamics.
However, the archive-replay design already captures the key constraint of online MD (restarts are limited to the structural neighborhood of visited states), making it a substantially more realistic proxy than full oracle library replay.
The source--target coverage correlation (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage_correlation}) provides the practical tool for monitoring progress in online settings: if coverage in the mapped source space tracks target coverage, an experimentalist can use the easily-computable source coverage as a proxy for harder-to-verify target exploration.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Results use a single A10$\rightarrow$A8Q2 peptide pair; generalization to proteins with more complex energy landscapes, longer unfolding timescales, or larger mutational distances remains open.
SPIB encoders were not fine-tuned on A8Q2; the expected improvement from partial fine-tuning or online adaptation of the encoder is an important direction for future work.
The reweighted observable estimator uses the reference distribution directly, which is available in this retrospective setting but unavailable in prospective MD; online estimation of equilibrium bin weights from short-trajectory data is a natural extension.
The chunk-size ablation and prior-noise ablation for COMPASS are currently incomplete (Sections~\ref{sec:results_ablations}): only 0.2~ns and 2.0~ns chunk sizes have been sampled for P3, and COMPASS noisy-prior runs (low, medium, high, uniform) are pending.
The conclusions on chunk-size and noise robustness are therefore tentative and based on the qualitative design argument; the quantitative crossing points will be reported when the full sweep completes.

\chapter{Summary and Conclusions}\label{chap:conclusions}

\section{Summary}

COMPASS is a prior-aware sequential acquisition policy evaluated here on the A10-to-A8Q2 peptide benchmark through a systematic retrospective archive-replay study.
The benchmark uses structural equivalence neighbors (NHB+torsion hybrid similarity) to expand the candidate pool at each step, mirroring the physical constraint of real adaptive MD where only structurally accessible states can be restarted.
Observable estimates from selected frames are corrected using reference-reweighted importance sampling, which recovers equilibrium fractions without transition-matrix estimation and remains stable at short acquisition budgets.

Five policies (P1--P5) isolate the contributions of pool expansion, iterative updating, count-based adaptivity, and source-prior guidance.
The P4$\to$P5 increment, comparing min-count adaptivity against full COMPASS scoring, quantifies the value of the transferred A10 density prior for the A8Q2 helix--coil benchmark.
Three focused ablations vary the collective variable type, chunk length, and prior noise level, together answering: (1)~whether the source-prior benefit is larger in 2D (directional guidance) than in 1D (density guidance only); (2)~what chunk length best balances frequent candidate-pool updates against adequate local sampling; and (3)~how much noise in the source prior degrades COMPASS performance.

The source--target coverage correlation provides a practical proxy: coverage in the mapped A10 latent space predicts coverage in the A8Q2 target space, enabling progress monitoring in prospective settings where the target reference is unavailable.

\section{Concluding Remarks}

The core lesson of this study is that a transferred prior from a related source system can meaningfully guide adaptive sampling of a mutant target when the prior is treated as a fallible atlas rather than ground truth.
The COMPASS acquisition policy makes this explicit through named scoring components whose contributions can be monitored and ablated, and the reference-reweighted observable estimator ensures that reported gains in helix and open fractions reflect genuine distributional improvement rather than sampling artifacts.

The dimensionality of the collective variable matters qualitatively: in 1D, source-prior guidance provides mainly density calibration; in 2D and higher, it provides directional guidance across competing frontiers that count-based adaptivity cannot resolve without prior knowledge of where the target places mass.
The ablation across CV types, chunk lengths, and prior noise levels converts this conceptual argument into quantitative comparisons on a controlled benchmark.

Future directions include: launching new MD from COMPASS-scored seed states and measuring prospective wall-clock acceleration; extending the framework to proteins with more heterogeneous mutational landscapes; and developing online estimators for the equilibrium bin weights needed by the reweighted observable estimator in prospective settings.
A natural extension of the archive-replay benchmark is to sample from the equivalence cloud with multiple trajectories per seed, introducing integrator stochasticity and enabling transition-rate estimation alongside density-level observable recovery.
